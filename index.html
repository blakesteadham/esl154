≈ 25% of exam · Competencies 001–002

≈ 45% of exam · Competencies 003–006

The heavyweight domain. ESL methods (sheltered instruction, TPR, communicative approaches), teaching listening/speaking/reading/writing, ELPS integration, content-area support, and assessment — including TELPAS and formative/summative/authentic assessment.

Domain III — Foundations, Culture, Family & Community

≈ 30% of exam · Competencies 007–009

Landmark court cases (Lau, Castañeda, Plyler), federal and Texas law (Title VI, TEC Chapter 89), LPAC responsibilities, program models, multicultural awareness, and family/community engagement.

Domain I deep review — Language & Acquisition (~25%)

The five subsystems of language▶
  • Phonology — the sound system. Phoneme = smallest sound that changes meaning (/b/at vs. /p/at). ELs struggle most with English sounds missing from their L1 (e.g., short vowels, final consonant clusters).
  • Morphology — word formation. Morpheme = smallest unit of meaning: un·help·ful = 3. Free morphemes stand alone (help); bound ones can't (un-, -ful). Teaching roots/affixes multiplies vocabulary.
  • Syntax — sentence structure and word order. L1 word order transfers: Spanish adjective-after-noun → "the car red."
  • Semantics — meaning: multiple-meaning words, idioms, figurative language. Idioms ("piece of cake") need explicit teaching.
  • Pragmatics — language in social context: politeness, turn-taking, register, indirect requests. "Can you pass the salt?" is a request, not a question.
Memory hook: Please Make Students Speak Properly — Phonology, Morphology, Syntax, Semantics, Pragmatics — ordered from sounds up to social use.
Krashen's five hypotheses▶
  • Acquisition vs. Learning — acquisition is subconscious, from meaningful communication; learning is conscious rule study. Acquisition drives fluency.
  • Comprehensible Input (i+1) — we acquire when input is one step beyond current level and made understandable (visuals, gestures, context). The most-tested hypothesis.
  • Affective Filter — anxiety, low confidence, and low motivation block input. Low-stress classrooms lower the filter. Scenario tell: a teacher creating a welcoming, mistake-friendly environment.
  • Monitor — learned rules only edit output when there's time and focus on form (editing an essay, not conversation).
  • Natural Order — grammar structures arrive in a predictable sequence no matter the teaching order, so developmental errors are expected.
Cummins: BICS vs. CALP + the iceberg▶

BICS (Basic Interpersonal Communication Skills): conversational, context-embedded, ~1–3 years. CALP (Cognitive Academic Language Proficiency): abstract, context-reduced academic language, ~5–7 years.

The classic scenario: a student who chats fluently but struggles with textbooks has BICS without full CALP — the answer is academic language support, never "he's unmotivated" or "retest his placement."

Cummins' Common Underlying Proficiency: skills learned in L1 (like reading comprehension) transfer to L2 — a key argument for bilingual education and for valuing the home language.

The five SLA stages + what the teacher does▶
  • 1. Preproduction (silent period, 0–6 mo): understands more than produces. → TPR, visuals, yes/no and pointing responses. Never force speech.
  • 2. Early production (6–12 mo): one/two-word answers. → Either/or questions, labeling, word banks.
  • 3. Speech emergence (1–3 yrs): short sentences, frequent errors. → Sentence stems, dialogues, recasting instead of overt correction.
  • 4. Intermediate fluency (3–5 yrs): longer, more complex speech; academic gaps remain. → Academic vocabulary, graphic organizers, structured writing.
  • 5. Advanced fluency (5–7+ yrs): near-native. → Continued academic refinement, idioms, register.
Exam pattern: match the activity to the stage. TPR for a newcomer = correct; demanding oral presentations from a preproduction student = wrong.
Learner language: transfer, interlanguage & friends▶
  • Positive transfer: L1 helps (cognates: familia/family). Negative transfer/interference: L1 hurts ("I have hunger").
  • Interlanguage: the learner's evolving in-between system — errors are data about where they are.
  • Overgeneralization: rule applied too broadly ("goed," "foots") — a healthy developmental sign.
  • Fossilization: errors that become permanent without feedback.
  • Code-switching: alternating languages mid-conversation — a bilingual skill, never a deficit. Any answer treating it as confusion is wrong.

Domain II deep review — Instruction & Assessment (~45%)

ELPS — who, what, where▶

The English Language Proficiency Standards are part of the required Texas curriculum for ELs in every subject and every grade. Responsibility belongs to all content-area teachers, not just the ESL teacher — a favorite exam question.

Teachers must post/deliver both a content objective and a language objective (listening, speaking, reading, or writing), and provide linguistically accommodated instruction matched to each student's proficiency level.

Methods you must recognize on sight▶
  • Sheltered instruction / SIOP: grade-level content made comprehensible — building background, visuals, adapted (not dumbed-down) text, content + language objectives. The default right answer for "teaching content to ELs."
  • TPR: commands + physical response. Beginners/silent period.
  • Language Experience Approach: students dictate a shared experience; teacher writes it; the text becomes reading material. Connects oral language to print.
  • Communicative Language Teaching: authentic interaction (role plays, information gaps) over drills.
  • Content-based instruction: language taught through real subject matter.
  • Natural approach: comprehension first, low anxiety, speech emerges when ready (Krashen & Terrell in practice).
The comprehensible-input toolkit▶

Slower (never louder) speech · gestures and facial expression · visuals, realia, demonstrations · shorter sentences · repetition and rephrasing · cognates · chunked instructions · frequent comprehension checks · word walls and anchor charts · wait time of 3–5+ seconds · structured talk (think-pair-share) with sentence stems.

Scenario heuristic: the correct strategy adds support without lowering the cognitive level. Wrong answers simplify the content, remove interaction, or isolate the student.
Literacy & the four language domains▶

Oral language is the foundation for EL literacy — build listening/speaking alongside reading/writing, never sequentially "speech first, print years later." L1 literacy transfers: a student who reads in Spanish doesn't relearn how to read, only the new code.

  • Pre-reading: build background knowledge and pre-teach key vocabulary.
  • During: shared/echo reading, picture walks, graphic organizers.
  • Writing: model → shared writing → guided → independent, with stems and word banks as scaffolds that fade.
Assessment: formative, summative, authentic + TELPAS▶
  • Formative: ongoing, low-stakes, used to adjust instruction (observation checklists, exit tickets, retells). If a question asks how to guide teaching, the formative option wins.
  • Summative: end-of-unit/year evaluation (unit tests, state exams).
  • Authentic/performance: real-world tasks — portfolios, oral retellings, projects. Fairest picture of what ELs can actually do.
  • TELPAS: annual, K–12, four domains (listening, speaking, reading, writing). Ratings: Beginning → Intermediate → Advanced → Advanced High. Results inform instruction, accommodations, and reclassification.
  • Linguistic accommodations (LPAC-determined for state tests): extra time, bilingual dictionary/glossary, clarified directions, oral support where allowed.

Domain III deep review — Foundations, Culture & Family (~30%)

Law timeline (memorize cold)▶
  • 1964 — Title VI, Civil Rights Act: no national-origin discrimination in federally funded programs. The legal root of everything below.
  • 1968 — Bilingual Education Act (ESEA Title VII): first federal funding for bilingual programs.
  • 1974 — Lau v. Nichols: identical instruction ≠ equal opportunity; schools must take affirmative steps for ELs.
  • 1981 — Castañeda v. Pickard: three-prong program test — (1) sound educational theory, (2) adequate resources/implementation, (3) evaluation showing results.
  • 1982 — Plyler v. Doe: free public education cannot be denied based on immigration status.
  • ESSA / Title III (current): federal funds + accountability for ELs' English proficiency progress.
Keep Lau and Plyler straight: Lau = language access, Plyler = immigration status. Castañeda = the 3-prong test (theory, implementation, results).
Texas specifics: TEC Chapter 89 & the LPAC▶

The 20-student rule: a district must offer bilingual education at the elementary level when it has 20+ ELs of the same language classification in the same grade level district-wide. Below that (or beyond elementary), ESL programs are required for every identified EL.

LPAC lifecycle: Home Language Survey at enrollment → any non-English answer triggers an English proficiency assessment → LPAC identifies and places the student within the first four weeks, with parental approval → reviews progress annually → sets state-testing accommodations → decides reclassification/exit → monitors exited students.

Program models: dual language (one-way/two-way — bilingualism + biliteracy goals), transitional bilingual (early/late exit — L1 support fading), content-based ESL, ESL pull-out.

Culture: shock, acculturation & funds of knowledge▶
  • Culture shock stages: honeymoon → hostility/frustration → adjustment → adaptation. A newcomer who turns withdrawn or irritable after an enthusiastic start is in the hostility stage — respond with routines, empathy, and peer connection, not discipline or referral.
  • Acculturation (adapt while keeping home culture, additive) vs. assimilation (replace it, subtractive). Schools should support acculturation.
  • Additive bilingualism — L2 added while L1 grows — predicts stronger outcomes. Any answer that devalues the home language is wrong.
  • Funds of knowledge (Moll): families' skills and experiences are academic resources — invite them into instruction.
Family & community engagement▶

Communicate in the home language: interpreters, translated documents, and program information parents can actually understand — parental approval for placement requires it. Offer flexible meeting times, home-school connections, and community partnerships. Treat families as partners with expertise, not as an audience to fix.

How to pick the "best" answer

Most questions describe a classroom scenario and ask for the best teacher response. When two options look right, run this filter:

The four-question filter▶
  • Is it comprehensible? Right answers add visuals, modeling, and context. Wrong ones just repeat, speak louder, or lecture.
  • Is it additive? Right answers value and use the home language and culture. Anything punishing L1 use or pushing assimilation is wrong.
  • Is it stage-appropriate? Match the demand to the proficiency level. Forcing speech from a silent-period student is always wrong.
  • Does it keep expectations high? Right answers scaffold grade-level content. Watering down content, excusing students from activities, or isolating them with worksheets is wrong.
Also beware of "always/never/only" in answer choices, and remember: developmental errors (goed, foots) call for modeling and recasting — not referral, penalty, or alarm.
